# Low-attention sports on Polymarket US: is there an exploitable edge for a slow taker bot?

Scope: tennis (ATP/WTA, ATP qualifying, ITF M15/M25/W15+, UTR Pro Tennis Tour), table tennis (Setka Cup Ukraine/Czechia/Moldova, Setka women, Czech Liga Pro, TT Elite, TT Cup, WTT), esports (CS2, LoL, Dota 2, Valorant), soccer from the EPL/La Liga/Ligue 1/UCL down to the EFL Championship, Argentine Primera Nacional (arg2), J2 and the English National League, MLB/NPB, AHL/KHL, cricket (T20I, IPL), darts (PDC, Modus), boxing/UFC, and the BETER "eBattles" efootball/ebasketball products. Everything in the "measured here" findings below was computed on this machine from the venue's own closed-events listing and minute-by-minute price history between 2026-10-24 22:00 UTC and 2026-10-25 00:05 UTC; supporting tables, the merged position and tape files, the live top-of-book poll, and the scripts are in `research_notes/Polymarket US worker edge survey/low_attention_sports_data/` (`analysis_tables.txt`, `positions.csv`, `tape_stats.csv`, `live_bbo_poll_2026-10-24.jsonl`, `replay.py`, `replay2.py`, `analyze.py`, `bbo_poll.py`). Nothing in the repository was modified; the module's own gitignored history cache under `data/raw/polymarket_us/history/` gained the newly read tapes.

## Key question 0: What was measured on the venue's own data, and how far can it be trusted?

### Takeaway
About 6,200 settled games across 45 league slugs were replayed with the repository's favorites-rule backtest (`atlas/polymarket/backtest.py`: `games`, `history`, `crossings`, `summary`) on the venue's one-minute price history, giving 48,110 paper positions; the tape carries each side's best ask only, so spreads and quote-change rates are measurable but depth is not, and "in play" had to be redefined by elapsed time because the venue's recorded finish time is unreliable for the low-tier leagues.

### Cited Findings
- The cached closed-events listing (`data/raw/polymarket_us/closed_events.json`, fetched 2026-10-24 22:52 UTC) holds 161,783 closed events over 381 league slugs. The largest low-tier slugs: setkameua 25,238; itfme 20,128; itfwo 15,875; atp 8,345; cs2 5,751; setkamecz 5,265; setkamemd 3,798; ebfsa 3,288; mlb 2,587; utr 2,395; czechligapro 2,062; itfm 2,034; wtt 1,824; lol 1,812; itfw 1,724; ebfpl 1,312; modus 1,075; dota2 1,064; ttelite 1,016; ufc 555; ebb 534; ttcup 457; pdcdarts 356; arg2 265; lal 182; epl 162; boxing 138; eflch 133; t20icr 120; ahl 113; eurolg 70; lg1 65; ipl 52; j2 50; engnl 47. — measured here (`replay.py` log, `analysis_tables.txt`)
- The slimmed cache keeps a moneyline only when its `sportsMarketType` is `moneyline` or ends in `_team_full_game_winner`; the low-tier sports use `tennis_match_winner`, `table_tennis_match_winner`, `soccer_team_full_time_winner` / `drawable_outcome` (one binary market per outcome), so 90 to 100 percent of ITF, Setka, UTR, soccer, darts and cricket events in the cache have no market attached. `replay2.py` re-read each sampled event in full from `GET /v1/events/{id}` (the moneyline's slug is `aec-` + the event slug) and used the market's own `feeCoefficient` and settled side prices. — measured here
- Sample: the newest 120 to 150 settled games per slug (two team-win markets per soccer match, the draw dropped): 6,228 games with a tape, 48,110 positions, 6,046 distinct events. Positions were deduplicated across the overlapping runs on (event, side, phase, threshold). — measured here
- Fee coefficients observed on the markets themselves: 0.0695 everywhere except table tennis, where Setka Cup (Ukraine, Czechia, Moldova, women) markets carry 0.10 and WTT 0.10 on 94 of 120 markets. The venue's published fee schedule (effective 1:00 PM ET, 19 October 2026) confirms `Fee = Θ × C × p × (1 − p)` with Θ = 0.0695 standard and Θ = 0.10 for Table Tennis, and no maker rebate below $10M of prior-month maker volume. — [Polymarket US fee schedule](https://docs.polymarket.us/fees.md); measured here (`*_meta.csv`)
- The venue's recorded finish time (`finishedTimestamp`/`closedTime`) is hours or days after the match for many low-tier markets: median start-to-finish 3,884 and 3,812 minutes for itfm and itfw (the June 2026 batches), 9,710 for ATP qualifying, 9,320 for T20I cricket, 2,018 for TT Cup, 3,415 for TT Elite, versus 131 for ATP and 20 to 31 for Setka Cup. For 24 percent of itfme tapes, 50 percent of cs2 and 41 percent of lol, the tape never prints a settlement price inside the read window. So the module's `minutes_left` is unusable for these leagues; the analysis instead uses `minutes_in` with a per-league cap on match length (tennis tours 180, ITF 150, UTR 120, Setka 40, esports 150 to 180, soccer 120, eBattles 25, cricket 220 to 240, darts 40 to 60, combat 40 to 60) to split "in match" from "dead book" (unsettled market after the match should have ended). — measured here (`tape_stats.csv`, `analyze.py`)
- The tape's terminal price agrees with the settled side price in 91 to 100 percent of games where a terminal price prints (e.g., ahl 113/113, nhl 150/150, itfm 130/130 of those that printed), so the long/short orientation of the history is consistent with settlement. — measured here (integrity check in the session log)

### Inferences
- The backtest's own caveats apply more strongly here than for the majors: a fill at the ask is assumed for the whole order, standard errors are clustered by game, and the low-tier samples are 30 to 300 positions per cell, so anything inside ±2 standard errors is noise.
- The "dead book" split is a knowable-live proxy, not ground truth: a tennis match that runs past 150 minutes or a BO3 esports series past 150 minutes is misclassified as dead; this is why esports shows a positive "dead book" cell at 0.85 (see Q4).

### Gaps
- Depth is not in the history; only a 145-second live poll of eight matches (Q1) says anything about size.
- The sample per league is the newest 120 to 150 settled games (one to 40 weeks of calendar depending on the slug), not the full listing; running the full listing is a matter of time (0.3 to 0.6 s per game) and would tighten the standard errors by roughly a factor of 3 to 10 for the big slugs.

## Key question 1: Who makes the prices on these markets, how deep are the books, and how often do quotes update in play?

### Takeaway
The in-play books on ITF, Setka Cup, ATP/WTA, CS2/LoL and top soccer are one-cent wide, reprice most minutes (and in the live poll every 5 to 10 seconds), and show tens of thousands of resting shares on each side, which is the signature of an automated professional maker fed from odds, not of retail; the exceptions are Czech Liga Pro, PDC/Modus darts, AHL/KHL, WTT/TT Elite/TT Cup and the eBasketball products, whose books are 8 to 32 cents wide most of the time and essentially never tight.

### Inferences
- The 1¢ spreads, six-figure resting share totals and 5-to-10-second repricing on ITF and Setka matches are not retail; they are consistent with one or more automated makers (Jump, the in-house desk, or program participants) pricing from a sportsbook/odds feed. The tiny reward pools ($17 to $52 per period over 300+ markets) mean the makers are not there for rewards; they are there because they can quote from a feed at low risk, which is the opposite of the "unmaintained book" hypothesis.
- The genuinely unmaintained books (Czech Liga Pro, darts, TT Elite/TT Cup/WTT, AHL/KHL, eBasketball) are unmaintained in the sense of being 8 to 32 cents wide; a taker crossing such a spread pays the whole width, so the favorites rule's own tight-book filter (≤ 4¢) excludes them almost entirely (0 to 13 percent of in-play minutes qualify). There is no "stale 1¢ book" anywhere in the sample.

### Gaps
- The gateway does not expose who the counterparty is; the attribution to a professional maker is inferred from quote behaviour and the Jump/in-house desk reports, not observed.
- Depth was polled for 145 seconds on eight matches; whether `askShares` of 110,000 is executable at the top or spread over 43 levels is not known from the field alone.

## Key question 2: Is there a favorite-longshot bias in low-tier tennis, table tennis, lower-league soccer and esports, and does it survive the fee?

### Takeaway
The literature finds a modest favorite-longshot bias in tennis that is larger between lower-ranked players, but on this venue's tape buying in-play favorites at 80 to 97 cents on a tight book earns between −2 and +1 points against cost in every low-tier group with more than 300 positions (ITF, Setka Cup, esports, ATP/WTA), i.e., nothing after a fee that costs 0.3 to 1.2 points per contract; the only positive cells with any sample are cricket (+4 to +5 points, n = 71 to 73) and lower-league soccer (+2 to +4.5, n = 44 to 80), both driven by one or two leagues and both small enough that a run of bad luck would erase them.

### Cited Findings
- Abinzano, Muga and Santamaría (2016) find the favorite-longshot bias on order-driven tennis exchanges, "larger in matches between lower-ranked players, in later-round matches and in high-profile tournaments", with results similar to Lahvička's bookmaker study, so bookmaker risk management cannot be the main driver. — [Applied Economics Letters 23(8)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/apeclt/v23y2016i8p605-608.html)
- Lahvička (2013), about 45,000 professional singles matches at bookmakers: the bias is larger in matches between lower-ranked players, in later rounds and at high-profile tournaments, which he reads as bookmakers protecting themselves against informed insiders and against the public exploiting new information. — [MPRA 47905](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/47905/1/MPRA_paper_47905.pdf)
- Forrest and McHale (2007) found FLB over three ATP seasons; Cain, Law and Peel (2003) found none in 91 Wimbledon matches. — [summarised in an Erasmus thesis](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/13970/)
- Lyócsa and Výrost (2018), testing 40 tennis betting rules with data-snooping corrections, conclude the tennis market "appears to be much more efficient than suggested by previous studies" and that evidence of profitable rules largely disappears. — [Applied Economics, via Masaryk University](https://muni.cz/en/research/publications/1397002)
- Soccer: Oikonomidis, Bruce and Johnson (2015) analysed 52,865 matches across 22 leagues in 11 countries and attributed cross-country differences in efficiency to league competitiveness; a 2019 thesis over ten European competitions (2013 to 2018) found FLB in almost all of them with no significant difference in returns between popular and less popular competitions; Daunhawer et al. (2017) found a strategy against the bias only marginally profitable across many bookmakers. — [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/373110/1/football_20efficiency_20full_20060115.pdf) (abstract via search; PDF returned 403); [Radboud thesis](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/handle/123456789/7858); [Manchester](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/biases-in-the-football-betting-market/)
- Esports: a Journal of Intelligent & Fuzzy Systems paper on LoL, CS:GO, Dota 2 and King of Glory reports that both the favorite-longshot and the reverse bias occur, with "completely different" distributions across titles and tournaments; for LoL the direction depends on match format and regional tournaments. — [abstract index](https://ip.ios.semcs.net/articles/journal-of-intelligent-and-fuzzy-systems/ifs232932) (host unreachable at fetch time; abstract from the search index only)
- No academic study of table-tennis betting efficiency (Setka Cup, Liga Pro, TT Cup) was found in three searches; the closest material is the tennis literature above. — gap, see below

### Inferences
- On the venue, the in-play favorite at ≥ 85¢ is priced about right in every thick low-tier market; the fee (0.0695 × p(1 − p) ≈ 0.9¢ at 85¢, 0.3¢ at 95¢; 1.3¢ and 0.5¢ on Setka at Θ = 0.10) is roughly the size of the whole measured deviation, so nothing survives it with a margin.
- The pregame numbers point, if anywhere, to a reverse favorite-longshot bias on this venue in tennis and table tennis (favorites overpriced at kick-off), which is the opposite of the bookmaker literature and would favour laying favorites, not buying them; with cells of 80 to 250 games the standard errors are 3 to 5 points, so this is a lead for the full listing, not a result.
- The cricket and EFL Championship cells are the only ones a bot could point at; both are "the leader with the bat/ball or a late lead holds" situations and both are too small to distinguish from luck (a 71-of-71 win record has a one-sided 95 percent lower bound of about 95.8 percent, which equals the 95.9 percent break-even).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of table tennis betting prices exists in the sources found; the Setka result above is the only measurement.
- The esports paper's full text (titles, sample, magnitudes) could not be retrieved.

## Key question 3: Integrity. What do match-fixing records in ITF/UTR tennis, Setka Cup table tennis, esports and the virtual products imply for a bot trading against whoever posts prices there?

### Takeaway
Tennis integrity alerts concentrate in the exact tiers in question (in 2025, 44 of the ITIA's 68 alerts came from ITF M15/M25/W15 to W75 events and the rest from Challengers, none from the ATP Tour), Setka Cup has a documented 2020 regulator alert and 365 player disqualifications, and ESIC's CS2 enforcement is almost entirely tier 2 and 3; for a taker bot this is pure adverse selection, because informed money moves the book before the bot's 45-second poll and the bot buys the favorite the insiders are laying.

### Inferences
- The informed flow in these tiers is on the lay side of favorites (the fix is almost always the favorite losing or a margin being missed). A bot that buys favorites at the ask, 45 seconds after the book moved, is the natural counterparty to that flow; the measured in-match edges of −1 to −8 points in itfm, UTR and Setka Moldova are consistent with, though not proof of, that selection.
- The maker's defence (widening or pulling quotes on suspicious flow) also removes the tight book the bot needs, so the fixing risk shows up for the bot as fewer fills rather than as catastrophic losses, except in the dead-book window where the loss is explicit.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how often Polymarket US voids or settles at $0.50 low-tier markets after an integrity alert; the rules text covers only pre-first-point retirements.
- The ITIA figures are alerts, not confirmed fixes; the fraction of ITF matches on the venue that were later sanctioned cannot be computed from public data.

## Key question 4: In-play latency. Is a 45-second poller structurally too slow for tennis and table tennis, and are there phases where a stale ask persists for minutes?

### Takeaway
Yes on the first count: the live books repriced within 5 to 10 seconds in the poll and every minute on the tape, the one-minute stale-limit replay fills only 46 percent of table-tennis crossings and 60 to 65 percent elsewhere, and the fills that do happen are the adversely selected ones; and no on the second: asks above 0.95 persist for a median of 0 to 2 minutes in Setka Cup and 2 to 8 minutes in tennis before settlement, and the long-lived stale asks that do exist (unsettled ITF markets open for days) lose heavily rather than paying off.

### Cited Findings
- The UK Gambling Commission describes courtsiding as transmitting live information faster than the official feed, notes that exchanges apply an in-play bet delay "to protect its bettors" while sportsbooks do so "to ensure their own prices are correct", and that the delay varies with "the potential latency of the data source". Industry reporting puts live streams 5 to 8 seconds behind the event and Betfair in-play delays at 1 to 12 seconds per market (`betDelay`). Imperial College researchers showed the tennis score can be inferred from live Betfair match-odds data alone when liquidity is sufficient, i.e., exchange prices move point by point. — [Gambling Commission](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/licensees-and-businesses/guide/in-play-or-in-running-betting); [SBC News](https://sbcnews.co.uk/latestnews/2026/05/20/cost-stream-delay-live-betting/); [techyorker (secondary)](https://techyorker.com/the-engineering-behind-live-in-play-odds/); [Dumitrescu et al.](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~wjk/publications/DumitrescuHuangKnottenbeltSpaniasWozniak.pdf)
- Easton and Uylangco (2010, International Journal of Forecasting; SSRN 2008) compared a point-by-point win-probability model with Betfair in-play odds for men's and women's matches: "a high level of efficiency in the betting market"; odds anticipate service breaks up to four points early, but players' tendency to lose more points after conceding a break is not priced in immediately, with no comparable bias after holds. — [Crossref record, DOI 10.1016/j.ijforecast.2009.10.004](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Forecasting+outcomes+in+tennis+matches+using+within-match+betting+markets+Easton+Uylangco&rows=2); [SSRN 1137766](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1137766)

### Inferences
- In tennis and table tennis the price moves every point and the maker reprices in seconds; a 45-second poll sees a price that is one to two points old on average, so the bot's fills are concentrated in the minutes when the favorite has just lost a point (ask unchanged or lower) and miss the minutes when it has just won one. That is why the filled set in the one-minute replay underperforms the ideal fill in the fast sports and why the table-tennis fill rate is under 50 percent.
- The "between sets / match point" phases where a stale ask might persist do not show up on this venue: the 0.95+ run before settlement is zero to two minutes in table tennis, which is shorter than the poll interval plus order latency. In tennis the 2-to-8-minute run exists but the late-phase tight-book edge is negative, so what persists is a fairly priced 95 to 97 cent ask, not a mispriced one.
- The one durable "stale" structure, ITF markets left open for hours or days, prices the wrong side a third of the time at any spread and still loses 4 to 8 points on a tight book, consistent with suspended or retirement-affected markets rather than a free option.

### Gaps
- The tape is one price per minute, so sub-minute staleness (the few-second windows the esports bot used) cannot be measured from history; the 145-second poll is the only direct evidence and covers eight matches.
- Whether the venue suspends or halts low-tier markets between sets (the API has SUSPENDED and HALTED states) was not observable; no ended-but-unsettled market was live during the probe. — [Order Book API overview](https://docs.polymarket.us/institutional/orderbook/overview)

## Key question 5: Pregame. Do these markets lag sportsbook consensus before the start, and would buying when the venue is cheaper than a sportsbook fair price have an edge after the fee?

### Takeaway
Pregame books on the low-tier sports are placeholders until shortly before the start (both asks at 0.97 to 0.98 on ITF listings two hours out; median pregame spread 57 to 82 cents on efootball and TT Cup), then converge to a one-cent book at kick-off whose calibration is, if anything, tilted against favorites in tennis and table tennis; no sportsbook benchmark could be compared here, and the literature's open-vs-close evidence says any pregame edge is in the opening line and disappears at the close.

### Cited Findings
- Median absolute move of the long ask between two hours before the start and the start, and the 90th percentile: ITF 2 to 3 cents / 31 to 46 cents; Setka Czechia 4 / 35; Setka Moldova 6.5 / 25; UTR 1 / 27; cs2 1 / 19; dota2 3 / 23; eBasketball 0 to 1 / 43 to 51; efootball (ebfpl, ebfsa) 44 to 49 / 62 to 66 with a median pregame spread of 57 to 73 cents; TT Cup 7.5 / 53 with a median pregame spread of 82 cents and a 27.5-cent spread at kick-off; TT Elite 25.5 / 53 with a 45.5-cent spread at kick-off; ATP/WTA, EPL, La Liga, UCL, MLB, NBA, NFL 0.5 to 1 / 1 to 6. — measured here ("Pregame move" table)

- A 2015 study of NFL and NBA spreads found opening lines exploitable but closing lines efficient after commission; a Southampton paper attributes between-league efficiency differences in soccer to competitiveness; practitioner material treats Pinnacle's closing line as the benchmark. ### Inferences
- Because the kick-off book is one cent wide and the maker is feed-anchored, a "venue cheaper than sportsbook fair" signal would fire mainly in the placeholder phase (hours out), where the venue's asks are wide or absent and the bot would be crossing a 20 to 80 cent spread, or in the last minutes when the maker's feed lags; the latter is a seconds-scale race the 45-second poller cannot win.
- The pregame reverse-FLB hint in tennis and table tennis is worth checking on the full listing because, if real, it points at buying the venue's longshot (or laying the favorite) before the start, which a taker at the ask can do; the measured cells are too small to act on.

### Gaps
- No sportsbook price series was available in this session (the repository tracks sportsbook closes only for the four majors), so opening-vs-closing value on the low-tier leagues versus a sharp book is unmeasured.
- UTR pregame tapes are often empty (no quotes until the start), so the UTR pregame cells are 2 to 15 positions.

## Key question 6: Capacity. With $1 to $25 orders and a $135 account, does any of this scale to meaningful dollars?

### Takeaway
Even taking the two positive groups at face value (cricket at about +4 points on 95-cent favorites, EFL Championship at +2 to +6 points), the venue produced about 5 to 11 tight in-match crossings per week per league in the sample, so $25 per crossing at a 3-point edge is roughly $4 to $8 a week before adverse selection; the depth exists (tens of thousands of shares on ITF and Setka books) but the edge does not, and on table tennis the 0.10 fee coefficient makes the bot's 0.0695 model understate costs by 44 percent.

### Cited Findings
- Tight in-match crossings at the 0.90 threshold per calendar week in the sample, and how many of them a one-minute-late limit filled: T20I cricket 5.4 / 3.9; IPL (season only) 7.6 / 4.6; EFL Championship 10.7 / 6.6; arg2 8.1 / 6.4; J2 8.4 / 5.3; English National League 13.9 / 10.4; UCL 14.0 / 8.6; La Liga 8.0 / 5.3; EPL 6.2 / 3.5; cs2 7.0 / 4.4; lol 8.7 / 5.9; ATP 13.5 / 8.3; WTA 12.8 / 8.1; itfwo 10.5 / 6.4; itfme 5.8 / 3.4 (the June ITF batches ran at 210 to 220 per week, the Setka slugs at 140 to 1,350 per week, UTR at 108 per week). — measured here (`analyze` session output; sample spans 0.07 to 42 weeks per slug)
- Live depth on the matches polled: 73,000 to 130,000 resting shares per side on ITF and Setka Cup Ukraine match-winner books, 44,000 to 58,000 on a CS2 map market, 770 ask shares on a Czech Liga Pro market; open interest 8,482 and 15,294 shares traded on one live ITF M15 match. — measured here (`live_bbo_poll_2026-10-24.jsonl`)
- Fee at the prices in question: Θ = 0.0695 gives 0.89¢ at 85¢, 0.63¢ at 90¢ and 0.33¢ at 95¢ per contract; Θ = 0.10 (Setka, WTT) gives 1.28¢, 0.90¢ and 0.48¢. Taker rebates start at $250,000 of prior-month taker volume (3 percent); maker rebates start at $10M. — [Polymarket US fee schedule](https://docs.polymarket.us/fees.md)
- The public gateway allows 25 requests per second shared across endpoints, and the docs recommend the `/v1/ws/markets` BBO stream (`SUBSCRIPTION_TYPE_MARKET_DATA_LITE`) over polling. — [Rate limits](https://docs.polymarket.us/api-reference/rate-limits.md)
- The one documented large in-play profit on Polymarket (about $208k in three months on LoL/Dota 2) came from seconds-scale data ingestion, not from a slow poller, and the author expects it to decay. — [esports.net](https://www.esports.net/news/polymarket-bot-makes-over-200k-in-3-months-with-lol-dota-2-esports/)

### Inferences
- Capacity is not the binding constraint anywhere in the thick low-tier books (ITF, Setka, esports, top soccer): $25 orders are invisible against 10^5 resting shares. The binding constraint is edge: at −2 to +1 points the expected value of a $25 order is between −50¢ and +25¢ before adverse selection.
- Where the measured edge is positive (cricket, EFL Championship), a $135 account placing $25 per signal at 5 to 11 signals a week and +2 to +4 points nets roughly $2 to $11 a week, with a realistic chance that the edge is zero; the account would need two to three orders of magnitude more signals (the full Setka/ITF firehose) and a positive edge there, which the sample does not show.
- The wide-book leagues (darts, Czech Liga Pro, TT Elite/Cup, AHL/KHL, eBasketball) have the opposite problem: the ask is there but it is 8 to 32 cents above fair, so a taker's "capacity" is capacity to donate the spread.

### Gaps
- Signal rates are from samples whose calendar spans range from hours (Setka Ukraine: 120 games in 11 hours) to ten months (NFL), so the per-week figures for the firehose leagues are extrapolations from a day or two of play.
- No live fill data for $1 to $25 IOC orders at the ask on these markets exists in the repository for the low-tier slugs; the stale-limit replay is the only execution model.
