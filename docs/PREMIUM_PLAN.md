# Atlas Sports Intelligence — Free and Premium

---

## The principle

Atlas sells **depth**, never **honesty**.

Everything that tells a reader how much to trust Atlas is free: the grade
letter, the entire reliability section, the methodology and the public
calibration record. Everything that makes the card faster and richer to work
with is premium.

Paywalling the caveat while giving away the claim is what a picks service
does. It is also, commercially, the wrong trade: the free tier's job is to
make the paid tier believable, and the reliability record is the only thing
that does that.

## Tiers

### Free

| Surface | What is included |
|---|---|
| Homepage | today's board, every game, search, filters |
| Matchup card | header, current spread and total, projected spread and total, **grade letter**, top driver, **all of §8 Reliability** |
| Team pages | season profile, recent form, current schedule |
| Research | methodology, the public calibration record, the live CLV tracker |
| Social | every card graphic Atlas posts |

The free card is genuinely useful. A reader gets the market number, Atlas's
projection, a grade for how much it is worth, and the seven-season record
behind that grade. That is more honest information than most paid products
provide.

## Why these lines

**The grade letter is free, the breakdown is paid.** The letter is the product
positioning in one character — it must be in front of everyone, including the
people who only ever see a screenshot. *Why* it is a B rather than an A is
analytical depth, and depth is the product.

**§8 is entirely free, forever.** It is the evidence. A reliability record
that costs money is not a reliability record, it is a claim.

**§4 Difference is entirely paid.** It is the section closest to being
misread as a recommendation, and it carries the most caveats. Putting it
behind a tier where the reader has context and has read the grade is a
product-safety decision as much as a commercial one.

**Opening numbers are paid.** They are the expensive part of the data
operation — hourly polling, stored history, a committed audit trail — and the
part with genuine scarcity.

**Team pages are free, team *history* is paid.** A reader looking up "how good
is Utah" should never hit a wall. A reader comparing four seasons of
opponent-adjusted splits is doing research.

## Shape of the offer

| | Free | Premium |
|---|---|---|
| Price | £0 | one monthly price, one annual price |
| Trial | — | full access during one football week, no card required |
| Seats | — | single user; no tiers, no add-ons, no upsells |

**No tiers above premium.** A "pro" tier invites the question of what is being
withheld, and the honest answer would have to be nothing.

**No pay-per-card.** It would create an incentive to make certain cards look
more valuable than others, which is precisely the corruption the grade exists
to prevent.

**No affiliate revenue, ever.** Books pay for traffic that converts to
deposits. Taking that money would mean Atlas earns more when readers act,
which is an interest directly opposed to the product's only claim. This is
worth stating in the marketing, because every competitor takes it.

## Anti-dark-patterns

These are rules, not aspirations.

<!-- lang-lint: quoting -->
| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| Blur a number under a paywall | teasing a figure is the tout playbook |
| Put the grade behind the wall | the grade is the honesty |
| Put §8 behind the wall | the record is the evidence |
| Email a "card of the day" | that is a selection with extra steps |
| Count down to kickoff | Atlas never hurries anyone |
| Hide the cancel link | — |
| Charge for the free tier's data by throttling | — |
<!-- lang-lint: end -->

Premium sections show a one-line description of what is behind them and a
single link. No blur, no ghosted digits, no "unlock to see if Atlas agrees".

## What premium must never become

The commercial pressure on this product will always be in one direction:
readers will ask which side to take, and the conversion rate on answering that
is high.

The answer is no, permanently, and the reason is structural rather than
ethical. Atlas's research established that it has no edge worth acting on. A
paid product that implied otherwise would be selling something Atlas has
measured and knows is not there — and the reliability record on the free tier
would contradict it in public, on every card.

The moment Atlas publishes a side, the grade becomes marketing and the
reliability record becomes a liability rather than an asset. There is no
version of this product that survives that.

## Measuring whether it works

| Signal | Healthy | Warning |
|---|---|---|
| Free → premium conversion | steady | spikes after a loud F-grade card — readers may be reading it as a recommendation |
| Which sections drive upgrades | §6 drivers, §7 movement | §4 difference — suggests it is being read as a recommendation |
| Cancellations | low and steady | clustered after a week of low grades — suggests the grade is being read as a forecast of Atlas's *accuracy this week* rather than of card quality |
| Support questions | methodology, data | "which side" — the positioning is not landing |

Any of the warning signals is a content and copy problem, not a pricing
problem, and the fix is on the card rather than in the checkout.
