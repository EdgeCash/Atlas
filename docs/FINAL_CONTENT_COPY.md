The bold clause is load-bearing. It is the sentence that decides whether
everything below it is read as research or as a tip sheet.

### What Atlas is

> **A research desk, published**
>
> Atlas models 58 college football games a week from a point-in-time database —
> every figure uses only what was knowable before kickoff, going back seven
> seasons.
>
> The model is anchored to the betting market, because seven seasons of
> out-of-sample testing said the market is the better starting point. Where
> Atlas differs from it, the card says by how much and what is driving it.
>
> And then it does the thing nothing else in this category does: it grades
> itself, in public, on every card, using its own historical record.

### What Atlas is not

> - **Not a selections service.** No card names a side. Not as a lean, not as
>   an arrow, not as a highlighted row.
> - **Not a sportsbook.** Nothing here can be acted on from this page, and
>   nothing is sized.
> - **Not a record of wins and losses.** The record Atlas publishes is
>   calibration: what it claimed, against what it delivered.
> - **Not urgent.** No countdowns, no alerts, nothing on any Atlas page moves.

### Why Atlas exists

> Every model in this category publishes its numbers with the same confidence
> every week. None of them tells you which of those numbers has historically
> been worth anything.
>
> Atlas measured that, and the answer was uncomfortable: **the further its
> model sits from the market, the worse it does.** Across seven seasons out of
> sample, cards claiming 77% accuracy delivered 50%. The loudest cards are the
> weakest ones.
>
> Most products would bury that. Atlas made it the largest element on the card.
> A grade that can say F is the only kind of grade worth anything, and it is
> the reason the A means something too.

## Research page — **live**

> A grade is **not** a recommendation. It answers one question: how much weight
> does the information on this card deserve?

> The six thresholds were chosen once, from the distribution of scores across
> seven seasons, and then fixed. They are **absolute**: a card's letter depends
> on that card and on nothing else on the board, so the same card grades the
> same on a quiet Tuesday and on championship Saturday. Atlas does not grade on
> a curve, because a curve would make a screenshot mean something different
> depending on the week it was taken.

> **A top grade is not a signal to read that card first.** Cards where Atlas and
> the market agree to within a point have realised 50.8% against a 51.3% claim
> across 760 games — statistically a coin flip. They grade highest because they
> are the most reliable, and they are the most reliable because Atlas has added
> nothing to them.
