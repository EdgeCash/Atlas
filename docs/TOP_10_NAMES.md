# Atlas — Top 10

**Phase 5: the logo test.** For each — logo concept, tagline, homepage feel,
social card feel — and the one line that decides it.

The deciding line is always the same sentence, because it is the sentence the
whole product rests on:

> *"{Name} sits 11.2 points from the market. {Name} marks its own card down."*

---

## 1 · Atlas — 41

**Logo** The letterform **A**, set in the UI sans at 680 weight, on the
off-white ground. No icon — `BRAND_GUIDE.md` already ruled that an abstract
mark would say nothing the wordmark does not, and a globe-and-shoulders logo
would be a pun the company lives with forever. The only illustrative option
worth considering is a single graticule corner — two map grid lines meeting —
which reads as *reference* without drawing a Titan.

**Tagline** Research. Analytics. Context. *(shipped, and it holds)*

**Homepage** Institutional calm. A wordmark, a week heading, and games. The
name does no work on the page because it does not have to — it is already
understood.

**Social card** `Atlas Sports Intelligence` top-left, `RESEARCH · ANALYTICS ·
CONTEXT` top-right. Five letters in the corner of a card whose conclusion is an
**F**. The brevity is the point: a long name beside a bad grade looks
defensive.

**The line** *"Atlas sits 11.2 points from the market. Atlas marks its own card
down."* — this already ships and is the best sentence in the product.

---

## 2 · Brier — 42

**Logo** A lowercase **b** whose bowl is drawn as a filled arc — a probability
mass. Or the wordmark alone, which is cleaner.

**Tagline** How much is this number worth?

**Homepage** Quieter and more technical than Atlas. The name invites a
"why Brier?" link, and that link is genuinely interesting: the Brier score is
how forecasting has measured calibration since 1950, and the grade is a Brier
score wearing a letter.

**Social card** `Brier` alone, five letters, very clean. But a reader seeing it
for the first time learns nothing, and most readers only ever see this.

**The line** *"Brier sits 11.2 points from the market."* Reads as a desk or a
surname — which is right.

**Why it does not win** It is a name for people who already know what
calibration is. The product is explicitly built for a sports fan who does not.

---

## 3 · Lodestar — 40

**Logo** A four-point star, thin-stroked, sitting where a compass rose's north
point would be. Or the wordmark with the **o** as a hairline star. The mark is
available in a way Atlas's is not.

**Tagline** The number you navigate by. *(or keep Research. Analytics.
Context.)*

**Homepage** Warmer than Atlas, slightly more literary. Same board, same cards.
The name carries a promise of orientation, which is the right promise — Atlas
tells you where you stand, not where to go.

**Social card** `Lodestar` is eight letters, one more line of wordmark. Still
clean, slightly less punchy beside a large **F**.

**The line** *"Lodestar sits 11.2 points from the market. Lodestar marks its
own card down."* Reads well. Slightly more poetic than Atlas, which is neither
better nor worse.

**Why it is the runner-up** Everything Atlas does, with a defensibility score
twice as high and no Liga MX club. It loses on brevity, on immediate
comprehension, and on the fact that a lodestar is a thing you follow — a small
but real friction for a product whose whole discipline is *not* telling anyone
where to go.

---

## 4 · Almanac — 38

**Logo** A wordmark with a hairline rule above and below, like a masthead. Or
**The Almanac** with the definite article set smaller — The Athletic's trick,
and it works.

**Tagline** Every game, every season, with the record to check it.

**Homepage** Editorial and seasonal. This name wants a yearbook, which suits
the reliability record and the archive — the two things
`ATLAS_FEATURE_ROADMAP.md` puts first.

**Social card** `The Almanac` reads warm and trustworthy. Possibly *too* warm —
a bad grade is a hard judgement and an almanac is a gentle object.

**The line** *"The Almanac sits 11.2 points from the market."* Awkward. An
almanac is a book; books do not sit near markets. **Agency is weaker than the
gate suggested.**

---

## 5 · Assay — 38

**Logo** A crucible mark, or a wordmark with a hairline under the two **a**s
like an assay stamp on metal.

**Tagline** Every number, tested.

**Homepage** Severe and expensive. This is a hallmarking office, not a sports
site.

**Social card** Four letters, superb. Nobody knows what it means.

**The line** *"Assay sits 11.2 points from the market."* Reads oddly — assay is
a verb first. **Weaker agency than scored.**

---

## 6 · Calibre — 36

**Logo** A wordmark with the **i** drawn as a fine measuring line.

**Tagline** Measured confidence.

**Homepage** Precise, slightly cold, quite British in spelling — and the
American spelling *Caliber* fragments the brand across two markets.

**Social card** Clean. The gun-bore association is quiet but present and it is
the wrong register for a product avoiding aggression.

**The line** *"Calibre sits 11.2 points from the market."* Works.

---

## 7 · Provenance — 36

**Logo** A wordmark only; the word is long enough to be the mark.

**Tagline** Where every number came from.

**Homepage** Auction-house premium. The most *expensive*-feeling name here.

**Social card** Ten letters and four syllables is a lot beside a grade.

**The line** *"Provenance sits 11.2 points from the market."* Does not work —
provenance is a property, not an agent.

---

## 8 · Mercator — 36

**Logo** A single meridian curve. The map family, kept.

**Tagline** Research. Analytics. Context.

**Homepage** Same as Atlas, one degree more scholarly.

**Social card** Good. Eight letters, distinctive.

**The line** *"Mercator projects 52.3"* — and "projects" is a map-projection
pun, which is charming.

**The disqualifier** The Mercator projection is famous, specifically, for
**distorting** what it represents. For a product whose only claim is accuracy,
the most widely known fact about its namesake is a distortion problem. Found
late; decisive.

---

## 9 · Reckoning — 36

**Logo** A wordmark; the **k** could carry a small angle mark.

**Tagline** Dead reckoning for the season.

**Homepage** Dramatic. "Dead reckoning" — navigating from a known reference by
measurement — is a *perfect* description of a market-anchored model, and "a
reckoning" is accountability. The double meaning is the best in the study.

**Social card** Strong, and slightly ominous.

**The line** *"Reckoning sits 11.2 points from the market."* Works.

**Why not** The tone is portentous, and this is a product that has spent six
sprints removing urgency from every surface. A name that sounds like a
consequence is at odds with a product that never tells anyone to act.

---

## 10 · Bellwether — 34

**Logo** Wordmark. Any sheep is a disaster.

**Tagline** The number that moves first.

**Homepage** Financial-press feel.

**Social card** Ten letters, three syllables, fine.

**The line** *"Bellwether sits 11.2 points from the market."* Works.

**Why not** A bellwether *leads*. Atlas explicitly follows the market and says
so. The name claims the opposite of the research finding the product is built
on.

---

## What the logo test changed

Three names that passed the written agency gate failed it **in a headline**:
Almanac, Assay and Provenance. Reading *"{Name} marks its own card down"* aloud
in a wordmark is a stricter test than reading it in a table, and it is the test
that matters, because that sentence is on every card.

Two names were eliminated on **meaning discovered late**: Mercator (distortion)
and Bellwether (leads rather than follows). Both would have survived a purely
numeric process, which is the argument for doing Phase 5 at all.

**To the final five:** Atlas, Lodestar, Brier, Almanac, Calibre.
